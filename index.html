01 · Robotics · Machine learning

Language-guided 3D targeting

Tell a robot arm “the cube left of the red ball,” and it finds that point in 3D and reaches for it. I built it in MuJoCo around the SO-101 arm, then used it to answer a training question: is reinforcement learning worth more than simply more fine-tuning?

93.7%within 2 cm, fine-tuned model (best seed)
94.0%within 2 cm, color + stereo (dev set)
+37.2 ptsproper fine-tuning vs one epoch (97.5% CI +33.8 to +40.6)
2Bparameters, trained on free Kaggle T4s
The full story

The arm sees the table the way current robots do: a stereo head camera, a wrist camera and its own joint angles. I derived the camera intrinsics and frame transforms and checked them against rendered depth. Then I compared two ways to get from words to a 3D point: a classical pipeline (color detection, stereo depth, inverse kinematics) and a 2B-parameter vision-language model (Qwen2-VL, QLoRA) fine-tuned to point at the target in the image, which stereo depth then lifts to 3D. Asked for x, y, z directly, the same model managed only 16%.

Given extra GPU time, is reinforcement learning (GRPO) a better use of it than more supervised fine-tuning? I froze the test sets and wrote the claims down before training, so no result could be tuned after the fact. More fine-tuning won. From an undertrained start it beat GRPO by about 21 points, and training the model properly first took it from 55.5% to 93.7% and 91.7% of targets within 2 cm (two seeds). Whether RL adds anything after that is not established: every RL run I continued from the well-trained model ended up keeping the unchanged starting point.

In-distribution scenes, error under 20 mm, in simulation. The model’s numbers are on a frozen confirmation set, scored once; the color + stereo baseline is on the development set, so the two aren’t a head-to-head. Learned models were scored on the point they output, without moving the arm.

Still sealed. The final test is blind: 430 encrypted prediction files, scored on free Kaggle GPUs under rules written down in advance. I haven’t opened the results yet.

Built with MuJoCo, Python, OpenCV stereo, inverse kinematics, Qwen2-VL, QLoRA, GRPO. The arm in the room solves the real SO-101 inverse kinematics for each target.

02 · Robotics · Purdue MARS Lab

MARSChair

A robot that sets up a conference room. In simulation, a mobile base brings tables out of storage, down a corridor and into place, then tucks two chairs under each one.

100 / 100room setups: a table and two chairs, hard floor and carpet
0.17 cmmedian table error (worst 0.35 cm, 0.33°)
49 / 50full layouts: 90 tables and 178 chairs placed
$989attachment, 19.3 kg, nine printed parts
The full story

I’m an undergraduate researcher in Purdue’s Mechanisms and Robotic Systems (MARS) Lab, with Prof. Yu She. The robot is the lab’s Fetch Freight base, and the furniture is the lab’s real furniture: flip-top tables on casters and sled-base chairs, modeled from their makers’ dimensions.

My first design used two arms. They were the wrong tool: each can push about 15–25 N, and a sled chair on carpet takes about 38 N to keep moving. The robot now has no arms at all, just one passive attachment on its front, built from 80/20 aluminum extrusion and nine 3D-printed parts. It drives its fork under a table to dock with the leg, and cradles a chair’s rear legs to push it. A hybrid A* planner with Dubins connections plans every move around what is already placed, a closed-loop tracker follows it, and a loaded robot never reverses or turns in place.

Friction, mass and start poses are randomized in every run. Chairs land within 1 cm of their slots.

The final attachment (Onshape). A finite-element check found the printed tongue at a safety factor of 1.0; an aluminum core took it to 7.1.

What this isn’t yet. All of it is simulation: nothing has run on the real robot, and the attachment is designed and costed, not built. The 100-run result is from the attachment just before the final one; the final model has passed 18 of 18 full runs.

Built with MuJoCo, Python, hybrid A*, Dubins paths, CadQuery, Onshape, finite-element analysis. 278 tests.

03 · Robotics · NASA RASC-AL 2027

HOPPER

A six-legged lunar rover that walks, and jumps when walking isn’t enough. I built the parametric CAD, the MuJoCo simulation and the design sweep for Purdue’s RASC-AL team.

2.31 mforward jump (1.59 m high, jumping straight up)
30°slopes climbed, at 0.38 m/s
0.50 m/swalking on flat ground, tripod gait
226 kgsix legs, 600 Wh battery, one MMRTG
The full story

RASC-AL is NASA’s university design competition, and this is our team’s concept for the 2027 “Enabling Extreme Exploration” theme. The rover stands on six three-jointed legs with a spring in each shin. To jump it crouches and winds the springs, lets go, and re-catches them on landing. A radioisotope generator keeps it powered where there is no sunlight.

I didn’t choose the design by hand. One set of parameters (leg count, battery size, generator) generates both the CAD and the simulation model, so every candidate can be built and tested the same way. A sweep of 105 designs picked the layout, and 1,741 simulated variants tuned it; 669 met every requirement.

What this isn’t yet. A concept, in simulation. Thermal behavior, radiation, dust, autonomy and soil mechanics are not verified, and many inputs are assumptions. The proposal is due in February 2027.

Built with MuJoCo, build123d, Python, NumPy, SciPy. The CAD check intersected 27,048 pairs of parts over 15 postures and found no overlaps.

04 · Machine learning · Interpretability

Steering a model by its neurons

Nudge 344 neurons in a small language model while it writes the first six tokens of an answer, and the whole answer comes out about a third shorter. I tested that the strict way, then handed the model the switch.

The full story

Lazy Genius

The model is Qwen3.5-4B. The edit is 344 neurons across its MLP layers: an 8-neuron core found by an automated search, plus 336 small corrections fit to undo the core’s mistakes. It is on for six tokens, then off, and the model finishes on its own.

To find out whether it really works, I ran two sealed tests. For each, the rules and the analysis script were hashed before the questions existed; 96 new questions were written by an agent that never saw the model’s output; and 16 blind graders (themselves language models) scored the answers without knowing which were edited. That is 1,536 graded answers.

Test 1Test 2
Words per answer208 → 131 (−37%)214 → 135 (−37%)
Core answer correct86.2% → 87.2% pass86.7% → 85.7% miss
Fully correct71.9% → 80.5% pass66.9% → 70.6% pass
Concision bar (30%)24.0% miss37.1% pass
VerdictFail, by one checkFail, by one check

Neither test was a clean pass, and that is the result. Test 1 missed its concision measure. Test 2 missed its accuracy bound by 0.2 points. The effect is real and specific, though: random neurons at the same dose changed length by 4% and 7%, and the same neurons pushed the other way made answers 24% and 16% longer.

Self-steer

Then I gave the model the switch. Qwen gets a tool that turns edits to its own MLP layers on and off in the middle of a conversation (switching costs about a microsecond), and when no edit fits, it can ask Claude to build one from a few contrasting examples: 3.9 seconds of compute for a new mode.

The question that mattered turned out to be who decides when the edit is on.

Who decidesAnswers shorterCore accuracy vs normal (points)
Claude routes each question20%+0.5

96 sealed questions × 2 seeds, blind-graded. Lines are 95% intervals.

What I concluded. The model is a poor judge of its own edits: left to choose, it cut too much and lost 13 points. Of the four modes Claude built, one clearly worked. For style, a one-line prompt did as well as or better than any neuron edit I made, and all three of my pre-registered follow-up hypotheses failed as written. The write-up says so.

06 · Mechanical · Side build

LEGO vacuum engine

A single-cylinder engine I built from LEGO that runs on the suction of a vacuum cleaner.

The vacuum pulls air out of the cylinder, and the air in the room pushes the piston in. A valve decides when the cylinder sees suction.

The valve is timed 90° from the crank, so it changes over as the piston reaches each end of its stroke. The flywheel carries the engine through each changeover.

07 · Research · Fluid mechanics

Passive vortex propulsion in air

My first-author IEEE paper. A rigid fin in a cylinder’s wake gets pushed forward by it: the first time this was shown in air.

+3.1 Nmean net thrust
p < 0.001t(19) = 15.75
CT 0.073at Re ≈ 3.3 × 10⁵
20 × 30 strials, 1.2 kHz
The full story

It started with my Honda Civic: could a car waste less energy in the air it leaves behind? Fish already do this. A trout behind a rock slaloms between the vortices it sheds and cuts its own muscle effort, but every passive-propulsion experiment I could find had been done in water.

So I built a rig to test it in air: a leaf blower through a fan to straighten the flow, a cylinder to shed the vortex street, and a 3D-printed NACA 0012 fin on a bearing and an MGN7 linear carriage. The carriage pressed on a 100 g load cell that I soldered, calibrated, and logged at 1.2 kHz.

The real rig, from the paper (Fig. 1). The bench under the display is modeled on it.
